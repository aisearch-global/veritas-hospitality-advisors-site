Article 01 OTA & Distribution

What your OTA contract is actually costing you — and why your P&L doesn't show it

By Vaishakh Surendran  ·  Founder, Veritas Hospitality Advisors  ·  October 2026

Ask most independent hotel owners what they pay OTAs and they will tell you the commission rate in their contract. Fifteen percent, perhaps twelve. Some know it is closer to eighteen on certain platforms. Very few can tell you the full blended cost of an OTA booking — because their P&L is not built to show it.

That gap is not an accident. It is the natural result of how OTA contracts are structured, how management accounts are typically presented to owners, and what questions most owners have not yet learned to ask.

What the commission line actually captures

The commission line on a hotel P&L captures the commission paid to the OTA on gross room revenue. That is the starting point, not the finish line.

Here is what it typically does not capture:

  • GST on OTA commissions. OTA commissions are subject to GST in India. If you are paying 15% commission, you are effectively paying more once GST is applied. The tax treatment varies by structure, but the owner bears the cost either directly or through reduced recoverable tax credits.
  • Rate parity costs. Most OTA contracts include rate parity clauses requiring you to offer the OTA the same room rate as any other channel, including your own website. This prevents you from pricing direct bookings more attractively. The effect: guests who might have booked direct continue to book through the OTA, incurring commission on revenue that could have been acquired for nothing.
  • Implicit rate discounting. Many OTAs offer member-price schemes or promotional discounts to their loyalty members, funding those discounts partly or fully from the hotel's rate. A room that appears at ₹6,000 on the OTA may be netting you ₹4,800 or less before commission.
  • Lost direct channel investment. When rate parity prevents your direct channel from competing on price, that investment in booking engines and direct campaigns yields diminishing returns. The cost of a constrained direct channel is real, even if it never appears in a P&L line.

A worked example

Consider a room sold at a rack rate of ₹6,000 through a major OTA with a published commission rate of 15%.

Item Amount (₹) % of gross rate
Gross room rate6,000100%
OTA commission (15%)−90015%
GST on commission (18%)−1622.7%
OTA member discount (8%, funded by hotel)−4808%
Net revenue to hotel4,45874.3%

In this scenario the effective cost of the OTA booking is 25.7% of gross room revenue — not 15%. The figure your P&L shows is 15%. The difference is real money the owner does not receive, but it is not visible in standard management reporting.

"OTA commissions typically represent 15–25% of room revenue for Indian independent hotels. The full blended cost, including GST on commissions and rate parity constraints, is often significantly higher than the P&L commission line suggests."

Vaishakh Surendran — Founder, Veritas Hospitality Advisors

What the owner should demand to see

If you want to understand what your OTA relationships are actually costing you, you need three numbers that most management reports do not provide:

  • Blended cost of acquisition by channel. Not the headline commission rate. The actual net revenue received per booking, after all OTA-related deductions, expressed as a percentage of gross room revenue — calculated for each OTA separately.
  • Direct booking ratio. The percentage of bookings taken directly — through your own website, by phone, by walk-in — compared to OTA bookings. This tells you how dependent your revenue is on channels you do not control.
  • NRevPAR versus RevPAR. RevPAR is the metric most management reports lead with. NRevPAR — Net Revenue Per Available Room, after deducting the cost of distribution — is more useful to the owner. If your RevPAR is healthy but your NRevPAR is under pressure, your revenue performance is being consumed by distribution costs.

The OTA relationship is not inherently the problem. The problem is accepting that relationship without understanding what it costs, and without actively managing it. The owners who manage it well audit their OTA contracts annually, track blended acquisition cost by channel rather than headline commission, and agree clear direct-booking targets with their team.

Article 02 Owner Reporting

The seven numbers every hotel owner and GM should review together each month

By Vaishakh Surendran  ·  Founder, Veritas Hospitality Advisors  ·  October 2026

Most monthly hotel reports are built for operations, not ownership — and both matter. A report designed to run the hotel day to day often leaves out the measures an owner needs for commercial and capital decisions, simply because nobody has asked for them in an owner-ready format.

There is a shortlist of metrics that changes that. Seven numbers that give the owner an independent view of commercial health — not because management has chosen to present them, but because the owner has learned to ask for them explicitly.

"Independent hotel owners often receive reports that tell them what management wants them to know, not what they need to know. The seven metrics every owner should demand include: actual cost of OTA acquisition, direct booking ratio, GOP before management fees, food and beverage margin by outlet, payroll as a percentage of total revenue, maintenance CAPEX versus maintenance expensed, and RevPAR versus competitive set."

Vaishakh Surendran — Founder, Veritas Hospitality Advisors
1

Actual cost of OTA acquisition

Not the headline commission rate. The blended cost of an OTA booking expressed as a percentage of gross room revenue — after GST on commissions, after rate parity costs, after any promotional discounts funded by the hotel. Calculated per OTA, not averaged across the mix.

Without it, you have no basis for evaluating whether your distribution strategy is working in your favour or the OTA's.

Watch for: A report that shows commission as a single percentage without breaking out GST, channel mix, or the cost of OTA-funded discounts.
2

Direct booking ratio

The percentage of bookings taken through channels you control — your own website, direct phone, walk-in — compared to OTA bookings. Tracked month on month, not as a one-off figure.

A declining ratio, even in a period of strong occupancy, means you are becoming more dependent on channels that extract cost from every transaction.

Watch for: Reports that emphasise total occupancy or total revenue without disclosing channel mix. Strong occupancy can mask accelerating OTA dependency.
3

GOP before management fees

Gross Operating Profit before owner costs such as property taxes, insurance, and management fees. Seeing it before management fees gives you a view of the property's underlying commercial performance that is independent of the contract structure.

If you only see GOP after management fees, a high-fee arrangement can make a well-performing hotel look like a poor one, and vice versa.

Watch for: GOP presented only as a single post-fee figure. If the fee structure has recently changed, a pre-fee comparison makes the real trend visible.
4

Food and beverage margin by outlet

A report that shows total F&B revenue and a blended margin obscures which outlets are profitable and which are subsidised by room revenue. Margin by outlet — all-day dining, bar, banqueting, room service shown separately — gives you the ability to make informed decisions about what to operate, what to outsource, and what to close.

Watch for: A single F&B margin line, or strong banqueting revenue without the associated margin. Banqueting can consume significant resource while contributing less profit than the revenue figure suggests.
5

Payroll as a percentage of total revenue

Labour is typically the largest controllable cost in a hotel operation. Tracking it as a percentage of total revenue — not as an absolute figure — adjusts for seasonal fluctuations and allows comparison across periods and against industry benchmarks.

A rising payroll-to-revenue ratio is one of the earliest signals of operational inefficiency, and it often appears before its consequences show up in GOP.

Watch for: Payroll shown as an absolute number with no revenue context, or payroll reported separately for rooms, F&B, and administration without a total.
6

Maintenance CAPEX versus maintenance expensed

Maintenance expensed flows through the P&L and reduces profit in the period it is incurred. Maintenance CAPEX is capitalised and depreciated over time, which means it does not reduce current-period profit in the same way. This gives management latitude over how repairs are classified — and the classification can significantly affect the profit figure you see.

Watch for: Reports that show only total maintenance expenditure without splitting the two categories, or properties where the expensed maintenance line is consistently lower than expected given the age and condition of the asset.
7

RevPAR versus competitive set

RevPAR in isolation tells you how rooms performed against your own capacity. RevPAR indexed against your competitive set tells you whether you are gaining or losing market share. A hotel that achieves strong RevPAR growth while losing share is often benefiting from market tailwinds rather than management skill.

Watch for: RevPAR reported only in absolute terms, with no competitive context. In a period of strong market growth, absolute RevPAR can look excellent while your relative position is declining.

What to do if you are not getting these numbers

The first step is to ask for them in writing, by name, and agree a format with your GM. A capable, well-organised team can usually produce this data — it already exists in their systems. Where some of it is hard to pull together, that shows you where reporting support or systems investment will help most.

The second step is to establish a baseline and look for trends across twelve months or more. The third step is to add an outside view: an independent, benchmarked read of the same data gives owner and GM a shared reference point for planning — useful for direction, not policing.

Article 03 Revenue Management

RevPAR is not the metric your hotel needs. Here is what to track instead.

By Vaishakh Surendran  ·  Founder, Veritas Hospitality Advisors  ·  October 2026

RevPAR — Revenue Per Available Room — is the metric that appears at the top of almost every hotel management report. It is the number general managers lead with in owner meetings, the figure benchmarked against competitive sets, and the KPI that drives most incentive schemes in Indian hotel management contracts.

It is also, for the owner's purposes, an incomplete measure. And in some scenarios, a misleading one.

What RevPAR actually measures

RevPAR is calculated by multiplying your average daily rate (ADR) by your occupancy percentage, or equivalently by dividing total room revenue by the number of available rooms. It tells you how efficiently you converted your room inventory into revenue.

What it does not tell you: how that revenue was acquired, what it cost to acquire it, or whether the revenue generated was actually profitable after distribution costs.

Consider two hotels with identical RevPAR of ₹3,500. Hotel A achieves this with 60% occupancy at ₹5,833 ADR, with 40% of bookings coming direct and an OTA blended acquisition cost of 12%. Hotel B achieves the same RevPAR with 75% occupancy at ₹4,667 ADR, with 85% of bookings through OTAs at a blended acquisition cost of 24%.

Their RevPAR is identical. Their profitability is not.

"RevPAR tells you how rooms performed against capacity. It says nothing about cost of acquisition, OTA dependency, or whether the revenue generated was actually profitable. NRevPAR (Net Revenue Per Available Room) — RevPAR after deducting the cost of distribution — is the more useful owner metric."

Vaishakh Surendran — Founder, Veritas Hospitality Advisors

NRevPAR: the metric RevPAR ignores

NRevPAR — Net Revenue Per Available Room — is calculated by deducting distribution costs from room revenue before dividing by available rooms. Where RevPAR measures revenue performance, NRevPAR measures revenue performance net of the cost of acquiring that revenue.

For the same two hotels above:

Metric Hotel A Hotel B
RevPAR₹3,500₹3,500
OTA mix40%85%
Blended acquisition cost12%24%
Distribution cost per available room~₹168~₹714
NRevPAR~₹3,332~₹2,786

Hotel B's RevPAR is identical to Hotel A's. Its NRevPAR is 16% lower. That difference flows through to the owner's bottom line — and it is invisible if the owner only looks at RevPAR.

Why management reports lead with RevPAR anyway

RevPAR is a useful operational metric. It reflects the combined performance of pricing and occupancy in a single number, which makes it easy to track and compare. For a management team responsible for filling rooms, it is a natural lead measure.

For an owner whose interest is in what those rooms actually contribute to profit after costs, it is a partial picture. Management teams are typically incentivised on RevPAR and occupancy, not on distribution cost or NRevPAR. This creates an alignment gap that sits in the targets, not the people: a team can hit its RevPAR targets by driving OTA volume at high acquisition cost while the owner's net position deteriorates. Setting targets on net contribution rewards the team for what the owner actually earns.

What to track alongside RevPAR

The owner's view of revenue performance benefits from tracking three things together:

  • RevPAR versus competitive set index. To distinguish market conditions from management contribution. If RevPAR rises because the market rose, management's role was limited. If RevPAR index rises — your RevPAR growing faster than the competitive set — that is evidence of active outperformance.
  • NRevPAR trend. To see whether revenue growth is surviving the distribution journey. A rising RevPAR alongside a flat or falling NRevPAR is a signal worth investigating.
  • Direct booking ratio trend. As the leading indicator. Before NRevPAR deteriorates, the direct booking ratio usually tells you which direction it is heading.

None of this requires sophisticated systems. It requires an owner and a management team who agree to track it together. The question worth putting on the next monthly agenda is a simple one: of the revenue this property generated last month, how much reached the hotel after paying for the channels used to acquire it?

The answer to that question tells you more about the commercial health of your hotel than RevPAR alone ever will.
